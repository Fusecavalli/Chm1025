## Unit 5 — Atomic Structure

### 1. Subatomic particles and isotope notation

| Particle | Symbol | Charge | Approximate relative mass | Location |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Proton | p⁺ | +1 | 1 u | Nucleus |
| Neutron | n⁰ | 0 | 1 u | Nucleus |
| Electron | e⁻ | −1 | 0.00055 u (about 1/1836 of a proton) | Electron cloud outside the nucleus |

For isotope notation **ᴬ_ZX** (or X–A):

- **Z = atomic number = number of protons.** Z identifies the element.
- **A = mass number = protons + neutrons.** A is a whole-number count, not the decimal atomic mass on the periodic table.
- Isotopes have the same Z but different neutron counts and mass numbers.

| Quantity | Formula |
| --- | --- |
| Protons | p = Z |
| Neutrons | n = A − Z |
| Electrons in a neutral atom | e = Z |
| Signed ion charge, in elementary-charge units | q = p − e |
| Electrons in an ion | e = Z − q |

Use the sign of q: a 2+ ion has q = +2 and two fewer electrons than protons; a 1− ion has q = −1 and one more electron. Gaining or losing electrons does not change the element or mass number.

### 2. Atomic mass from isotope abundances

**Average atomic mass = Σ(isotopic mass × fractional abundance)**

For two isotopes:

**m̄ = m₁f₁ + m₂f₂**, where **fᵢ = percent abundance / 100** and **f₁ + f₂ = 1**.

Procedure:

1. Convert every percentage to a decimal fraction.
2. Multiply each isotope's given mass in u by its fraction.
3. Add the products; round only the final result.
4. Check that the average lies between the isotope masses and closer to the more abundant isotope.

Use given **isotopic masses**, not integer mass numbers, when isotopic masses are supplied.

### 3. Periodic table, principal energy levels, and electron dots

- Elements are ordered by increasing atomic number. Groups are columns; periods are rows.
- Main-group elements in a group usually have similar chemical properties because they have the same number of valence electrons.
- Main-group valence counts: group 1 → 1; group 2 → 2; groups 13–18 → 3–8. **Helium is the exception: 2.**
- Metals tend to lose electrons; nonmetals tend to gain or share them. Noble gases have filled outer shells.

For **neutral atoms of the first 20 elements**, use these principal-shell populations, from the innermost shell outward. These are shell arrangements, not orbital-box diagrams.

| Element | Z | Electrons per principal shell | Valence electrons |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| H | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| He | 2 | 2 | 2 |
| Li | 3 | 2, 1 | 1 |
| Be | 4 | 2, 2 | 2 |
| B | 5 | 2, 3 | 3 |
| C | 6 | 2, 4 | 4 |
| N | 7 | 2, 5 | 5 |
| O | 8 | 2, 6 | 6 |
| F | 9 | 2, 7 | 7 |
| Ne | 10 | 2, 8 | 8 |
| Na | 11 | 2, 8, 1 | 1 |
| Mg | 12 | 2, 8, 2 | 2 |
| Al | 13 | 2, 8, 3 | 3 |
| Si | 14 | 2, 8, 4 | 4 |
| P | 15 | 2, 8, 5 | 5 |
| S | 16 | 2, 8, 6 | 6 |
| Cl | 17 | 2, 8, 7 | 7 |
| Ar | 18 | 2, 8, 8 | 8 |
| K | 19 | 2, 8, 8, 1 | 1 |
| Ca | 20 | 2, 8, 8, 2 | 2 |

For these atoms, the fourth shell starts filling at K; do not assume the third shell can hold only eight electrons in all elements.

**Electron-dot symbols:** Write the element symbol and add one dot per valence electron. Place one dot on each of four sides before pairing; most main-group symbols have up to eight dots. Helium has a filled first shell with two electrons.

### 4. Periodic trends

| Property | General change left → right across a period | General change top → bottom down a group |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Atomic radius | Decreases | Increases |
| First ionization energy (energy needed to remove an electron from a gaseous atom) | Increases | Decreases |
| Electronegativity (attraction for shared bonding electrons) | Increases | Decreases |

These are general trends with exceptions; noble gases often have no assigned electronegativity on introductory tables.

- A cation is smaller than its parent atom; an anion is larger than its parent atom.
- For ions with the **same electron count**, more protons generally mean a smaller radius. Do not use this shortcut for arbitrary ions with different occupied shells.

### Unit 5 practice — solve on paper first

**5.1 — Particle inventory (SLO3.3–3.4)**

A **neutral sulfur-34 atom** has atomic number **16**. Calculate its numbers of protons, neutrons, and electrons. Show the subtraction used to find neutrons. Then calculate the electron count after it gains two electrons to become S²⁻.

<details>
<summary>Reveal answer and calculation: 5.1</summary>

- Protons: p = Z = **16**.
- Neutrons: n = A − Z = 34 − 16 = **18**.
- Neutral-atom electrons: e = Z = **16**.
- S²⁻ electrons: e = Z − q = 16 − (−2) = **18**.
- Gaining electrons leaves the nucleus unchanged: the ion still has 16 protons and 18 neutrons.

</details>

**5.2 — Weighted atomic mass (SLO3.5)**

A hypothetical element X has exactly two naturally occurring isotopes:

| Isotope | Isotopic mass | Percent abundance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| X–63 | 62.930 u | 69.20% |
| X–65 | 64.928 u | 30.80% |

Calculate its average atomic mass in **u**, rounded to **three decimal places**. Treat the supplied values as the data for this exercise.

<details>
<summary>Reveal answer and calculation: 5.2</summary>

f₁ = 0.6920; f₂ = 0.3080; their sum is 1.0000.

m̄ = (62.930 × 0.6920) + (64.928 × 0.3080)

m̄ = 43.54756 + 19.997824 = 63.545384 u

**Average atomic mass = 63.545 u.** It lies between the two masses and is closer to the more abundant, lighter isotope.

</details>

## Unit 6 — Chemical Bonding

### 1. Ionic versus covalent bonding

| Feature | Ionic | Covalent/molecular |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bonding model | Electrostatic attraction between cations and anions; electron loss/gain explains simple ion formation | Atoms share electron pairs |
| Typical element combination | Metal + nonmetal; also compounds containing polyatomic ions such as NH₄⁺ | Nonmetal + nonmetal |
| Typical structure | Extended ionic lattice; formula gives an ion ratio | Discrete molecules for molecular compounds |
| Typical properties | Often high melting points; conducts when molten or when dissolved with mobile ions; solid does not conduct | Molecular substances often have lower melting points and poor electrical conductivity |

These are patterns, not universal rules. Some covalent substances form networks with high melting points. A polyatomic ion contains covalent bonds internally but forms an ionic compound with an oppositely charged ion.

**Octet rule:** Many main-group atoms gain, lose, or share electrons to reach eight outer-shell electrons; a filled first shell has two. Common simple-ion charges are group 1: +1; group 2: +2; Al: +3; group 16: −2; group 17: −1. Do not apply this pattern to assign Cu or Fe a single fixed charge.

### 3. Ionic naming and formula rules

**Name → formula:**

1. Identify the cation and anion, including each charge. A Roman numeral specifies a variable-charge metal's charge.
2. Choose the smallest positive whole-number ion counts satisfying **n₊q₊ + n₋q₋ = 0**; q₋ is negative.
3. Write the cation first and the anion second. Omit subscripts of 1 and omit ion charges from the neutral compound formula.
4. Put parentheses around a polyatomic ion only when more than one of it is required. Keep its internal subscripts unchanged.

Examples: calcium chloride → **CaCl₂**; aluminum oxide → **Al₂O₃**; calcium nitrate → **Ca(NO₃)₂**. Reduce the **ion ratio**, not the internal formula of a polyatomic ion.

**Formula → name:**

- Name the cation first, then the anion.
- A monatomic anion uses its element root + **-ide**: oxygen → oxide; chlorine → chloride.
- A polyatomic ion keeps its memorized name: sulfate stays sulfate; nitrate stays nitrate.
- For a variable-charge metal, infer its charge from neutrality and give a Roman numeral: FeCl₂ → **iron(II) chloride**; FeCl₃ → **iron(III) chloride**.
- For the fixed-charge metals in the required list (including Ag⁺ and Zn²⁺), use the ordinary element name without a Roman numeral in standard introductory naming.
- Do not use molecular counting prefixes such as *di-* or *tri-* for ionic compounds. A Roman numeral reports charge, not the number of metal atoms.

### 4. Binary molecular/covalent naming rules

For simple **binary molecular compounds** (two nonmetal elements):

1. Name the first element as written, using a prefix when more than one atom is present. Usually omit **mono-** on the first element.
2. Name the second element using a numerical prefix and the element root + **-ide**, including **mono-** when there is one atom.
3. Convert prefixes directly to subscripts when writing a formula. **Do not reduce a molecular formula** to an empirical ratio.
4. Learn common oxide contractions: **monoxide**, **tetroxide**, **pentoxide**.

| Atoms | Prefix | Atoms | Prefix |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | mono- | 6 | hexa- |
| 2 | di- | 7 | hepta- |
| 3 | tri- | 8 | octa- |
| 4 | tetra- | 9 | nona- |
| 5 | penta- | 10 | deca- |

Examples: CO → **carbon monoxide**; N₂O₄ → **dinitrogen tetroxide**; sulfur hexafluoride → **SF₆**.

### Unit 6 practice — solve on paper first

**6.1 — Charge balance and atom count (SLO4.4–4.5)**

Magnesium ions are **Mg²⁺** and phosphate ions are **PO₄³⁻**. Calculate the smallest whole-number counts of each ion needed for a neutral compound. Write the compound's formula and calculate the **total number of atoms in one formula unit**. Show the total positive and negative charges.

<details>
<summary>Reveal answer and calculation: 6.1</summary>

The least common multiple of 2 and 3 is 6.

- **3 magnesium ions** give 3(+2) = **+6**.
- **2 phosphate ions** give 2(−3) = **−6**.
- Net charge = +6 − 6 = 0.
- Formula: **Mg₃(PO₄)₂**, magnesium phosphate.
- Atom count: 3 Mg + 2 P + 8 O = **13 atoms per formula unit**.

Do not confuse the five constituent ions with the thirteen atoms.

</details>

**6.2 — Infer a metal charge and balance nitrate (SLO4.3–4.5)**

A copper ion contains **29 protons and 27 electrons**. Nitrate is **NO₃⁻**.

1. Calculate the copper ion's charge and state how many electrons the neutral atom lost.
2. Calculate how many nitrate ions are needed per copper ion for a neutral compound.
3. Write the compound's formula and name, then calculate the total atoms in one formula unit.

<details>
<summary>Reveal answer and calculation: 6.2</summary>

1. q = p − e = 29 − 27 = **+2**. The neutral copper atom lost **2 electrons**, forming Cu²⁺.
2. One Cu²⁺ needs **2 nitrate ions**: (+2) + 2(−1) = 0.
3. Formula: **Cu(NO₃)₂**. Name: **copper(II) nitrate**.
4. Atom count: 1 Cu + 2 N + 6 O = **9 atoms per formula unit**.

</details>

## Unit 7 — Lewis Structures

### 1. Electron-counting formulas

| Quantity | Working rule |
| --- | --- |
| Total available valence electrons, V | V = Σ(each atom's valence-electron contribution) − q, using the signed overall charge |
| Electrons in bonds | 2 × (number of single bonds + 2 × number of double bonds + 3 × number of triple bonds) |
| Remaining nonbonding electrons | V − electrons in bonds |
| Lone pairs | Nonbonding electrons / 2, for the closed-shell structures practiced here |
| Electronegativity difference | ΔEN = \|EN₁ − EN₂\| |

For a negative ion, subtracting q **adds** electrons; for a positive ion, it **removes** them. A single bond has one shared pair (2 electrons), a double bond two pairs (4), and a triple bond three pairs (6).

When checking an atom's octet, count **both electrons of every bond touching that atom**. When checking the structure's total electron inventory, count each shared pair **only once**.

### 2. Drawing simple covalent structures

1. Count the total available valence electrons.
2. Choose a skeleton: hydrogen is terminal; the less electronegative non-hydrogen atom is usually central.
3. Connect atoms with single bonds, using two electrons per bond.
4. Fill terminal-atom octets; hydrogen needs only a **duet** (two electrons).
5. Place remaining electrons on the central atom.
6. If the central atom lacks an octet, try converting adjacent lone pairs into double or triple bonds without changing the total electron count.
7. Check the total electron count and each atom's octet/duet. Enclose polyatomic ions in brackets with the overall charge when applicable.

For a simple ionic Lewis diagram, show separate bracketed ions and charges rather than a shared bond: a main-group cation commonly has lost its original valence electrons, and a simple anion commonly has a completed octet. Not all compounds obey the octet rule; use it for the simple examples covered here rather than forcing it onto every possible molecule.

### 3. Bond polarity

- **Nonpolar covalent bond:** equal or nearly equal sharing; identical bonded atoms have ΔEN = 0.
- **Polar covalent bond:** unequal sharing; the more electronegative atom is **δ−**, and the other is **δ+**.
- Larger ΔEN generally means more unequal sharing. Ionic and covalent bonding form a continuum.
- The syllabus supplies no numerical polarity cutoffs. Use any cutoffs and electronegativity table provided by your instructor or ALEKS rather than assuming a universal boundary.
- **Bond polarity does not by itself establish whole-molecule polarity.** Molecular shape and dipole cancellation are additional considerations outside the stated objectives.

### Unit 7 practice — solve on paper first

**7.1 — Electron budget and double bonds (SLO4.6–4.7)**

Draw the Lewis structure of **CO₂**, using **O–C–O** as the skeleton. Carbon contributes **4** valence electrons and each oxygen contributes **6**.

Calculate:

1. Total available valence electrons.
2. Total electrons in bonds in the completed octet-satisfying structure.
3. Total nonbonding electrons and total lone pairs.
4. Lone pairs on carbon and on each oxygen.

<details>
<summary>Reveal answer and calculation: 7.1</summary>

1. V = 4 + 2(6) = **16 valence electrons**.
2. The completed structure is **O=C=O**, with **two lone pairs on each O** and none on C. Two double bonds contain 2(4) = **8 bonding electrons** (4 shared pairs).
3. Nonbonding electrons = 16 − 8 = **8**; total lone pairs = 8/2 = **4**.
4. Carbon: **0 lone pairs**. Each oxygen: **2 lone pairs**.

Carbon has eight electrons around it from its two double bonds. Each oxygen has four bonding electrons plus four lone-pair electrons. The structure contains sixteen electrons in total, not twenty-four: shared electrons count around both bonded atoms but only once in the inventory.

</details>

**7.2 — Lone-pair count and electronegativity difference (SLO4.6–4.8)**

Draw the Lewis structure of **NH₃**, placing nitrogen in the center. Nitrogen contributes **5** valence electrons; each hydrogen contributes **1**. Use **EN(N) = 3.04** and **EN(H) = 2.20**.

Calculate:

1. Total available valence electrons.
2. Electrons used in the three N–H single bonds.
3. Remaining electrons and lone pairs on nitrogen.
4. ΔEN for each N–H bond, rounded to two decimal places. Classify the bonds as polar or nonpolar covalent and assign δ+ and δ−.

<details>
<summary>Reveal answer and calculation: 7.2</summary>

1. V = 5 + 3(1) = **8 valence electrons**.
2. Three single bonds use 3(2) = **6 bonding electrons**.
3. Remaining electrons = 8 − 6 = **2**: **1 lone pair on nitrogen**. The completed structure has three N–H single bonds and one lone pair on N. Nitrogen has an octet; each hydrogen has a duet.
4. ΔEN = |3.04 − 2.20| = **0.84**. The N–H bonds are **polar covalent**; nitrogen is **δ−**, and each hydrogen is **δ+**.

</details>
